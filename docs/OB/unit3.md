# Unit 3: Group Dynamics, Teamwork and Decision-Making

## 3.1 Group Dynamics

### Meaning of Group
A **group** is a collection of two or more individuals who interact with each other, share common goals, and perceive themselves as a distinct unit. Groups are fundamental building blocks of organizations.

### Group Behaviour
Group behaviour refers to the actions, interactions, and patterns of conduct that emerge when people work together in a group setting. It is influenced by group norms, roles, cohesion, and leadership.

### Group Dynamics
Group dynamics is the study of the forces operating within groups — how groups form, function, change, and affect individual members. It examines interactions, communication patterns, power structures, and decision-making processes.

**Key elements**:
- **Norms**: Informal rules that guide behaviour
- **Roles**: Expected behaviour patterns for members
- **Cohesion**: Degree of attraction and bonding
- **Status**: Relative rank or position within the group
- **Size**: Affects participation, performance, and dynamics

### Types of Groups

| Basis | Type | Description | Example |
|-------|------|-------------|---------|
| **Formal** | Command Group | Defined by organizational hierarchy | CEO and direct reports |
| | Task Group | Formed to accomplish a specific task | Project team, committee |
| **Informal** | Interest Group | Formed around a shared interest | Employee sports club |
| | Friendship Group | Based on personal relationships | Colleagues who lunch together |
| **Membership** | In-group | Group one belongs to | Your own team |
| | Out-group | Group one does not belong to | Another department |
| **Reference** | Reference Group | Group used for self-evaluation | Aspirational professional group |

### Five-Stage Model of Group Development (Tuckman's Model)

```
Forming ──→ Storming ──→ Norming ──→ Performing ──→ Adjourning
```

| Stage | Description | Key Behaviours | Leadership Role |
|-------|-------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **1. Forming** | Members get acquainted, establish ground rules | Polite, cautious, dependent on leader | Directive — provide clarity and structure |
| **2. Storming** | Conflicts arise over roles, goals, and methods | Disagreement, competition, resistance | Coaching — manage conflict, encourage expression |
| **3. Norming** | Consensus develops, norms and cohesion form | Cooperation, acceptance, shared responsibility | Facilitating — support team agreements |
| **4. Performing** | Group is fully functional and productive | High trust, efficient work, autonomy | Delegating — empower the team |
| **5. Adjourning** | Group disbands after achieving goals | Closure, reflection, transition | Supporting — acknowledge contributions |

## 3.2 Teamwork

### Definition of Teamwork
Teamwork is the collaborative effort of a group of people working together toward a common goal. It involves coordinated action, mutual accountability, and shared purpose.

### Benefits of Teamwork

| Benefit | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Synergy** | Combined output exceeds individual contributions (1+1 > 2) |
| **Diverse Perspectives** | Multiple viewpoints lead to better solutions |
| **Shared Workload** | Tasks are divided, reducing individual burden |
| **Learning** | Members learn from each other's skills and experience |
| **Innovation** | Collaboration sparks creativity and new ideas |
| **Motivation** | Social support increases commitment and morale |
| **Risk Mitigation** | Shared responsibility reduces individual pressure |

### Stages of Team Development
Same as Tuckman's Model (Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning), but in a team context the emphasis is on interdependence, shared goals, and collective accountability.

### Difference Between Groups and Teams

| Dimension | Group | Team |
|-----------|-------|------|
| **Purpose** | Share information | Collective performance |
| **Accountability** | Individual | Individual + Mutual |
| **Leadership** | Strong, focused leader | Shared leadership |
| **Output** | Sum of individual work | Collective work products |
| **Synergy** | Neutral or negative | Positive (1+1 > 2) |
| **Interdependence** | Low | High |
| **Goal** | Information sharing | Common purpose & goals |

### Types of Teams

| Type | Description | Example | Key Features |
|------|-------------|---------|--------------|
| **Functional Team** | Members from the same department or specialization | Accounting team, HR team | Single expertise, permanent |
| **Cross-Functional Team** | Members from different functions collaborate on a project | Product development team (engineering, marketing, design) | Diverse skills, time-bound |
| **Self-Managed Team** | Team responsible for planning, executing, and evaluating its own work | Autonomous factory floor teams | High autonomy, no formal supervisor |
| **Virtual Team** | Members are geographically distributed and communicate via technology | Global project team on video calls | Remote, tech-dependent, flexible |

### Problems Affecting Teamwork

| Problem | Description | Solution |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| **Social Loafing** | Members exert less effort in a group than alone | Clarify individual accountability, smaller teams |
| **Groupthink** | Desire for consensus suppresses dissent and critical thinking | Encourage devil's advocate, anonymous input |
| **Free Riding** | Some members contribute little but benefit from team's work | Peer evaluations, defined roles |
| **Process Loss** | Time and energy lost in coordination rather than production | Clear processes, effective communication tools |
| **Coordination Problems** | Misalignment of tasks, schedules, or responsibilities | Project management tools, regular check-ins |
| **Conflict** | Interpersonal or task disagreements that hinder progress | Conflict resolution training, facilitation |
| **Lack of Trust** | Members do not feel safe sharing ideas or admitting mistakes | Team building, transparency, psychological safety |

## 3.3 Effective Communication in Teams

### Importance of Open Communication
- Builds trust and psychological safety
- Prevents misunderstandings and errors
- Encourages sharing of ideas and feedback
- Enables quick problem-solving
- Strengthens relationships and cohesion

### Feedback in Teams

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Positive Feedback** | Reinforces effective behaviour | "Your presentation was clear and well-structured" |
| **Constructive Feedback** | Suggests improvement areas | "The report was thorough; adding a summary would make it more impactful" |
| **Peer Feedback** | Feedback exchanged among team members | Regular peer review sessions |
| **360-Degree Feedback** | Feedback from all directions (peers, subordinates, supervisors) | Annual performance reviews |

**Guidelines for Effective Feedback**:
- Be specific, not general
- Focus on behaviour, not personality
- Be timely — give feedback close to the event
- Use "I" statements ("I noticed..." rather than "You always...")
- Balance positive and constructive feedback
- Make it a dialogue, not a monologue

### Conflict Resolution in Teams
- Address conflict early before it escalates
- Focus on interests, not positions
- Separate people from the problem
- Use "I" statements to express concerns
- Seek win-win solutions through collaboration
- Involve a neutral facilitator if needed

## 3.4 Decision Making

### Definition
Decision making is the cognitive process of selecting a course of action from multiple alternatives to achieve a desired outcome. It involves identifying problems, gathering information, evaluating options, and committing to a choice.

### Importance of Decision Making
- Determines organizational direction and strategy
- Allocates resources effectively
- Solves problems and resolves conflicts
- Drives innovation and change
- Directly impacts performance, efficiency, and morale

### Types of Decisions

| Basis | Type | Description | Example |
|-------|------|-------------|---------|
| **Nature** | Programmed | Routine, repetitive, well-defined rules exist | Approving leave requests |
| | Non-Programmed | Novel, unstructured, no standard procedure | Entering a new market |
| **Level** | Strategic | Long-term, top management, high impact | Merger or acquisition |
| | Tactical | Medium-term, middle management | Department budget allocation |
| | Operational | Short-term, front-line management | Daily shift scheduling |
| **Certainty** | Certain | Complete information, known outcomes | Reordering office supplies |
| | Uncertain | Incomplete information, unknown outcomes | Launching a new product |
| | Risky | Known probabilities of outcomes | Investment decisions |

### Individual vs Group Decision Making

| Aspect | Individual | Group |
|--------|------------|-------|
| **Speed** | Faster | Slower |
| **Quality** | Limited by one person's knowledge | Broader expertise, better quality |
| **Creativity** | Narrower ideas | Diverse perspectives, more innovation |
| **Buy-in** | Lower commitment from others | Higher acceptance and ownership |
| **Risk** | May be overly cautious or overly bold | Groupthink can lead to poor decisions |
| **Accountability** | Clear — one person responsible | Diffused — harder to assign accountability |
| **Cost** | Low cost | Higher time and resource cost |

**When to use individual**: Simple/routine decisions, time-sensitive situations, when decision-maker has expertise.

**When to use group**: Complex/non-programmed decisions, when acceptance matters, when diverse input is needed.

## 3.5 Decision Making Processes & Tools

### Herbert Simon's Decision-Making Model

Simon's model describes three phases of decision making, with a focus on the bounded rationality of decision-makers.

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │                                                   │
   │    INTELLIGENCE           DESIGN          CHOICE  │
   │   (Identify the         (Develop          (Select  │
   │    problem &              alternatives)   the best)│
   │    gather data)                                  │
   │                                                   │
   │         │                    │                    │   ← Feedback
   │         ▼                    ▼                    ▼        loops
   │                                                   │
   │              IMPLEMENTATION & REVIEW               │
   │                                                   │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### Phase 1: Intelligence
- Identify and define the problem or opportunity
- Gather relevant data and information
- Scan the internal and external environment
- Recognize when a decision is needed

**Questions**: What is the problem? What information is available? What are the symptoms?

#### Phase 2: Design
- Develop possible alternative solutions
- Analyze feasibility and consequences
- Create evaluation criteria
- Consider constraints (time, resources, policies)

**Questions**: What are the options? What are the pros and cons of each?

#### Phase 3: Choice
- Evaluate alternatives against criteria
- Select the best course of action
- Make the decision and commit to it

**Questions**: Which option best meets our goals? What is the final decision?

#### Phase 4: Implementation & Review (added later)
- Put the decision into action
- Monitor outcomes and gather feedback
- Make adjustments if needed

### Bounded Rationality (Simon)
- Decision-makers have limited information, time, and cognitive capacity
- They cannot evaluate all possible alternatives
- Instead of seeking the **optimal** solution, they **satisfice** — choose the first acceptable option

### Other Decision-Making Tools

| Tool | Description | Best For |
|------|-------------|----------|
| **Rational Model** | Logical step-by-step: define, evaluate, select optimal | Programmed decisions with complete info |
| **Intuitive Model** | Decisions based on gut feeling and experience | Experienced managers under time pressure |
| **Decision Tree** | Visual map of choices and outcomes | Complex, sequential decisions |
| **Cost-Benefit Analysis** | Compare total costs vs benefits of each option | Resource allocation |
| **Pareto Analysis** | 80/20 rule — focus on the few vital causes | Prioritization |
| **Pros-Cons List** | Simple list of advantages and disadvantages | Personal or small-scale decisions |

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: The Cross-Functional Team That Couldn't Decide

**Scenario**: A software company forms a cross-functional team to develop a new mobile app. Members come from Engineering, Design, Marketing, and Sales. The team is stuck in the **Storming** stage — Engineers want to use a new tech stack, Designers want more features, Marketing wants a quick release, and Sales wants specific customer integrations. Meetings run overtime with no decisions. The project is 3 months behind schedule.

**Analysis**:
- **Group Development Stage**: Stuck in Storming — unresolved conflicts over goals and methods
- **Type of Team**: Cross-functional — diverse perspectives but difficult coordination
- **Decision Making Problem**: No structured process — endless debate without choice
- **Conflict**: Task conflict (approach) turning dysfunctional

**Recommended Solutions**:
- **Simon's Model**: Implement structured decision process — define problem (Intelligence), list viable options (Design), vote (Choice)
- **Norming**: Establish a shared goal and decision-making ground rules
- **Leadership**: Appoint a team lead to facilitate and drive decisions
- **Decision Tool**: Use decision tree to evaluate build approaches based on cost, time, and quality

### Case Study 2: Groupthink in the Boardroom

**Scenario**: A well-established company's executive board is discussing a major acquisition. The CEO strongly supports it. Other board members have private doubts but do not express them for fear of challenging the CEO. No one raises the red flags. The acquisition proceeds and fails spectacularly, costing the company millions.

**Analysis**:
- **Problem**: Groupthink — desire for consensus overrode critical evaluation
- **Group Type**: Command group (formal hierarchy)
- **Decision Type**: Strategic, non-programmed
- **Symptoms**: Illusion of unanimity, self-censorship, pressure on dissenters

**Recommended Solutions**:
- Assign a **devil's advocate** role in every major meeting
- Use **anonymous voting** for critical decisions
- Encourage **dialectical inquiry** — structured debate of opposing views
- Create **sub-groups** to evaluate options independently before full meeting
- Foster culture where challenging leadership is rewarded, not punished

### Case Study 3: The Virtual Team That Never Connected

**Scenario**: A global company creates a virtual team with members in India, the UK, and the US. After 6 months, the team is underperforming. Communication is limited to emails and weekly calls where only 2-3 people speak. Time zone differences cause delays. Trust is low. Members feel isolated and unclear about their roles.

**Analysis**:
- **Team Type**: Virtual team — geographic and cultural dispersion
- **Problems**: Poor communication, lack of trust, coordination problems, unclear roles
- **Group Development**: Stuck in Forming — never progressed to Norming or Performing
- **Communication**: No open dialogue, feedback lacking

**Recommended Solutions**:
- Use **asynchronous tools** (Slack, Trello, shared docs) alongside synchronous calls
- Rotate meeting times to share the inconvenience of odd hours
- Schedule **virtual team-building** activities
- Define **clear roles, goals, and communication norms**
- Create a **team charter** agreed upon by all members
- Use **360-degree feedback** to surface concerns

### Case Study 4: The Self-Managed Team That Fell Apart

**Scenario**: A manufacturing plant introduces self-managed teams. One team of 12 workers is given autonomy over production schedules, quality checks, and shift assignments. Initially it works well, but over time some members do less work (social loafing), decisions are delayed because everyone must agree, and stronger personalities dominate. Productivity drops below traditional team levels.

**Analysis**:
- **Team Type**: Self-managed team
- **Problems**: Social loafing, process loss, coordination problems, lack of clear accountability
- **Group Dynamics**: Norms and roles not properly established
- **Decision Making**: Consensus requirement caused paralysis

**Recommended Solutions**:
- Define **individual roles and responsibilities** within the team
- Implement **peer evaluation** to address social loafing
- Move from **consensus** to **majority vote** for routine decisions
- Provide **team training** on decision-making processes
- Keep teams smaller (5-7 members) for better coordination

### Case Study 5: The R&D Team's Innovation Dilemma

**Scenario**: An R&D team must decide between two projects: Project A — incremental improvement to an existing product (low risk, steady return) and Project B — radical innovation (high risk, potentially huge payoff). Half the team prefers A for stability, the other half prefers B for growth. The deadline for the decision is one week away.

**Analysis**:
- **Decision Type**: Non-programmed, strategic, uncertain
- **Conflict Type**: Task conflict (which project to pursue)
- **Group Development**: At Norming — basic trust exists, clear disagreement
- **Applicable Model**: Simon's model needed — Intelligence (analyze both projects), Design (evaluate scenarios), Choice (select)

**Recommended Solutions**:
- Use **cost-benefit analysis** and **decision tree** for both projects
- Apply **Pareto analysis** — identify 20% of factors driving 80% of value
- **Intelligence phase**: Gather data on market trends, costs, risks
- **Choice phase**: Use a weighted scoring matrix with agreed criteria
- If still split, escalate with recommendations to senior leadership

### Case Study 6: The Team That Avoided All Conflict

**Scenario**: A marketing team prides itself on being harmonious. No one ever disagrees in meetings. Suggestions from the team lead are accepted silently. However, after every meeting, small groups complain privately. Mistakes keep happening because problems are never discussed. Creative output is mediocre.

**Analysis**:
- **Group Development**: Stuck in Norming/False Harmony — skipped Storming
- **Problem**: Suppressed conflict — surface harmony masks underlying issues
- **Decision Making**: Groupthink and avoidance
- **Communication**: No open feedback, passive-aggressive behaviour

**Recommended Solutions**:
- Create **psychological safety** — explicitly invite dissent
- Use **anonymous suggestion** mechanisms
- Introduce **constructive conflict** as a team value
- Train the team on **giving and receiving feedback**
- The team lead should model **vulnerability** by admitting their own mistakes
- Hold **"safe space"** meetings where honest feedback is mandatory

## Additional Real-Life Exam Examples

### Example 7: Groupthink — NASA Challenger Disaster (1986)
**Scenario**: In 1986, NASA's Space Shuttle Challenger exploded 73 seconds after launch, killing all 7 crew members. The disaster was caused by O-ring failure in cold weather. Engineers from Morton Thiokol (the contractor) had warned that the O-rings were unsafe below 53°F, but NASA managers overruled them. The launch proceeded despite the warning.
**OB Analysis**: This is the textbook case of Groupthink. Symptoms included: Illusion of Invulnerability (NASA believed it had a perfect safety record), Self-Censorship (engineers did not escalate further after being overruled), Pressure on Dissenters (Thiokol managers told engineers to "take off their engineering hats and put on their management hats"), and Illusion of Unanimity (silence was taken as agreement). The decision was made in a command group with a strong leader pushing for launch.
**Exam Tip**: The Challenger disaster is THE most cited Groupthink example in OB exams worldwide. Memorize it.

### Example 8: Cross-Functional Team — Tesla's Model 3 Production
**Scenario**: When Tesla launched the Model 3, Elon Musk created a cross-functional "production hell" team combining engineers, designers, supply chain managers, and factory workers. They lived on the factory floor for months, sleeping in sleeping bags, to solve production bottlenecks. The team worked with extreme interdependence — design changes had immediate manufacturing implications, and supply chain issues required real-time engineering fixes.
**OB Analysis**: This cross-functional team demonstrates both the power and pain of diverse expertise. The intense collaboration eventually solved the production crisis, but at the cost of extreme burnout. Key success factor: a shared, compelling goal (save the company from bankruptcy) overrode functional silos.
**Exam Tip**: Tesla's Model 3 "production hell" team is a dramatic example of cross-functional teams under extreme pressure.

### Example 9: Virtual Team — GitLab's All-Remote Model
**Scenario**: GitLab, a DevOps platform, operates as a fully remote company with 2,000+ employees across 65+ countries. They have no physical offices. Everything — communication, decision-making, documentation — happens asynchronously via text. Their public handbook (4,000+ pages) documents every process, policy, and decision. They use planned "coordination calls" but default to async text. Employee retention is high, and productivity is competitive.
**OB Analysis**: GitLab solves virtual team challenges through: Extreme Documentation (replaces hallway conversations), Asynchronous First (respects time zones), Written Communication (over video calls), Transparency (any employee can see any decision), and Clear Norms (documented handbook). Their biggest challenge: building social bonds and trust without face-to-face interaction.
**Exam Tip**: GitLab is the world's largest all-remote company. Use it as the benchmark example for virtual team management in exams.

### Example 10: Social Loafing — Ringelmann's Rope Pull & Modern Context
**Scenario**: In the 1910s, French engineer Max Ringelmann asked people to pull a rope alone and in groups. He found that individuals pulled harder alone (85 kg) than in groups of 3 (65 kg each) or 8 (39 kg each). This phenomenon — Ringelmann Effect — is the classic demonstration of social loafing. Modern research confirms this in workplace settings: team members contribute 20-30% less effort in groups of 5+ compared to working individually.
**OB Analysis**: Social loafing increases with group size. Causes: Diffusion of Responsibility (no one person is accountable), Reduced Evaluation Apprehension (no individual performance measurement), and Sucker Effect (if others loaf, I'll loaf too). Solutions: clear individual accountability, smaller teams (4-5 people), peer evaluations, and meaningful tasks.
**Exam Tip**: Ringelmann's rope pull is the original experiment proving social loafing. Cite it with the numbers (85 kg alone vs 39 kg in group of 8).

### Example 11: Simon's Decision Model — Amazon's "Disagree and Commit"
**Scenario**: Amazon's leadership principle "Disagree and Commit" operationalizes Simon's model. When a decision is needed: Intelligence phase (data gathering, debate), Design phase (multiple options proposed, pros/cons analyzed), Choice phase (if consensus fails, the decision-maker chooses — but everyone commits to the decision regardless of disagreement). Jeff Bezos described it as: "Use data to make decisions, but when you're down to two good options, just pick one and move fast."
**OB Analysis**: Amazon avoids both analysis paralysis (waiting for perfect information) and groupthink (suppressing dissent). Teams debate vigorously (functional conflict) in the Intelligence and Design phases, but once a decision is made (Choice), everyone commits fully. This is Bounded Rationality in action — choose the satisficing option rather than seeking the optimal one.
**Exam Tip**: Amazon's "Disagree and Commit" is a brilliant applied example of Simon's Decision-Making Model in exams.

### Example 12: Group Norms — Google's "No Interruption" Meetings
**Scenario**: Google's "No Interruption" meeting norm: In brainstorming sessions, the first 10-15 minutes are silent — everyone writes ideas on sticky notes before any discussion begins. This prevents high-status or loud voices from dominating. Only after ideas are generated does the group discuss, cluster, and evaluate. This norm was created to combat production blocking (the tendency for one person's talking to block others' ideas).
**OB Analysis**: Group norms can be deliberately designed to improve group performance. Google's norm ensures that introverts and junior members contribute equally, that ideas are evaluated on merit not presenter status, and that creative output is maximized before critical evaluation begins.
**Exam Tip**: Google's silent brainstorming norm is a practical example of how group norms can be intentionally designed for better group dynamics.

### Example 13: Decision Making Under Uncertainty — COVID-19 Lockdown Decisions
**Scenario**: In March 2020, governments worldwide faced non-programmed decisions under extreme uncertainty — should they impose lockdowns? They had incomplete data about the virus, unknown economic impacts, and no historical precedent. India's 21-day lockdown (announced with 4 hours notice) was a decision under uncertainty, using Bounded Rationality (limited data, time pressure) and Satisficing (choose the best option with available information).
**OB Analysis**: This exemplifies all decision types: Non-Programmed (no precedent), Uncertain (unknown outcomes), Strategic (national level), with Group Decision Making (cabinet + health experts). The decision process followed Simon's model: Intelligence (case data from China/Italy), Design (lockdown vs no lockdown vs partial), Choice (full lockdown), Implementation (4-hour notice). India's choice was satisficing — not optimal, but the best possible with available information.
**Exam Tip**: COVID-19 lockdown decisions are the most contemporary example of Non-Programmed Decision Making under Uncertainty. Use it in any exam question on decision types.

### Example 14: Tuckman's Model in Startups — From Forming to Performing
**Scenario**: A typical Indian startup journey: In Forming (0-10 employees), everyone is polite and excited. Storming (10-50 employees) hits hard — founders disagree on direction, early hires feel entitled, new hires resist "corporate" processes, and conflicts erupt daily. Many startups die in this stage. Successful ones reach Norming (50-200 employees) by establishing clear roles, values, and processes. At Performing (200+ employees), the team executes efficiently, resolves conflicts quickly, and culture is self-sustaining.
**OB Analysis**: Startup failures are often blamed on product or funding, but the real cause is inability to progress past Storming. Founders need to adapt their leadership at each stage: Directive in Forming, Coaching in Storming, Facilitating in Norming, Delegating in Performing.
**Exam Tip**: Connect Tuckman's Model to the Indian startup lifecycle for a contemporary, relatable exam answer.

### Example 15: Team Communication Failure — Air France Flight 447
**Scenario**: In 2009, Air France Flight 447 crashed into the Atlantic, killing all 228 people. The cause was not mechanical failure but team communication failure. When the autopilot disengaged due to ice in the speed sensors, the junior co-pilot made an incorrect control input (pulling up instead of pushing down, causing the plane to stall). The senior co-pilot in the back did not realize what was happening. Neither clearly communicated. The captain was on a rest break and was not called until too late. Hierarchy prevented the junior co-pilot from clearly stating the crisis.
**OB Analysis**: This demonstrates the deadly cost of poor team communication. Hierarchy (Captain outranked both co-pilots) created reluctance to speak up. Lack of shared mental model (each pilot had a different understanding of the situation). Poor feedback and unclear role definition. The solution: Crew Resource Management (CRM) training that explicitly flattens hierarchy, encourages speaking up, and standardizes communication protocols.
**Exam Tip**: Air France 447 is the most dramatic example of team communication failure in any OB exam. It powerfully illustrates why psychological safety and open communication matter.

## Summary

- **Groups** go through Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning stages
- **Teams** differ from groups in interdependence, accountability, and collective output
- **Team types** include functional, cross-functional, self-managed, and virtual — each with unique challenges
- **Effective communication** requires open dialogue, structured feedback, and timely conflict resolution
- **Decision making** ranges from programmed to non-programmed; Simon's model (Intelligence → Design → Choice) provides a structured approach
- **Individual vs group decisions** — choose based on complexity, time, and need for buy-in
- **Common problems** (groupthink, social loafing, process loss) must be actively managed
